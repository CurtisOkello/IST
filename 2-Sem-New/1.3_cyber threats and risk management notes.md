# PART 1: The Current Cyber Threat Landscape

## 1.1 Why Study the Landscape?

A **threat landscape** is the collection of threats, threat actors, techniques and trends relevant to an organization, sector or region at a given time. Risk analysis is only as good as the threat information feeding it. If your threat picture is two years old, your **likelihood** estimates are guesses.

> **Analyst habit:** Always ask of any statistic: *Who measured it, over what period, from what sample, and what were they counting?*

## 1.2 What the 2025-2026 Reports Say

The figures below come from major annual reports. Treat them as **snapshots**, and always check the latest edition before quoting numbers.

### Key Findings at a Glance

| Source | Scope | Headline findings |
|--------|-------|-------------------|
| **Verizon DBIR 2026** (published May 2026) | 31,000+ incidents and 22,000+ confirmed breaches in 145 countries | **Exploitation of vulnerabilities (31%) overtook credential abuse (13%)** as the top initial access vector, the first time in the report's history; remediation is not keeping pace with the growth in vulnerabilities; third-party and supply chain risk keeps rising; employees pasting source code and sensitive documents into external AI tools is a measured data-leakage path |
| **IBM Cost of a Data Breach 2026** (published August 2026) | Breach cost study across many countries | Global average cost **$4.99M** (up 12%, a record); US average **$11.5M**; healthcare the most expensive sector for the 13th year at **$6.64M**; financial services **$6.29M**; mean time to identify and contain a breach rose to **247 days** (183 to identify, 64 to contain); about one in four malicious breaches was **AI-enabled**, costing roughly **$6M** on average; AI and automation in security operations saved roughly **$1.9M** per breach |
| **ENISA Threat Landscape 2025** (published October 2025) | 4,875 incidents in the EU, July 2024 to June 2025 | **Ransomware** is the most impactful threat; **hacktivist DDoS dominates incident volume** but only about 2% of incidents caused service disruption; state-nexus espionage continues against public administration; info-stealers (Lumma was assessed as the most prevalent) feed the criminal ecosystem; AI is increasingly used to scale phishing and social engineering |

### Reading the Numbers Critically

These reports appear to disagree, and learning why is a core analyst skill.

| Apparent conflict | Explanation |
|-------------------|-------------|
| DBIR says **exploitation** is the top initial access vector; IBM reports **phishing** as the leading initial attack vector | Different datasets and definitions. DBIR counts confirmed breaches from incident responders and partners; IBM surveys breached organizations and models cost. The "initial vector" may be recorded differently, and the DBIR notes that its credential figure counts only the **first recorded step** of a breach |
| ENISA: DDoS is the **most numerous** incident type but ransomware is the **most impactful** | **Volume is not impact.** Many low-impact incidents can outnumber a few devastating ones |
| Cost figures differ by region (US $11.5M vs global $4.99M) | Regulatory exposure, litigation, notification rules and detection costs vary by country |

> **Rule of thumb:** Use reports for **direction and priorities**, not as precise predictions for your organization.

## 1.3 Eight Trends Shaping the Landscape

### 1. Vulnerability Exploitation Is Accelerating

The gap between **disclosure** and **mass exploitation** keeps shrinking, while **patching speed** lags. Attackers prioritize internet-facing edge devices (VPNs, firewalls, file-transfer tools) because one flaw yields broad access. This reinforces earlier lessons: patching known flaws prevents a large share of real attacks, and **asset visibility** is the prerequisite.

### 2. Ransomware Is an Industry, Not a Gang

Modern ransomware is run as **Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS)**: developers supply the malware and leak site, **affiliates** perform intrusions, and negotiators and launderers handle payment. Tactics include **double extortion** (encrypt and threaten to leak) and pure **data-theft extortion** with no encryption at all. ENISA named strains such as Akira and SafePay among the most deployed.

### 3. Identity Is the New Perimeter

Stolen credentials, session cookies and tokens let attackers "log in rather than break in." **Info-stealer malware** harvests browser-saved passwords and session tokens at scale, and these logs are sold to initial access brokers. Defenses shift toward **phishing-resistant MFA**, conditional access and token protection.

### 4. Social Engineering Moves Beyond Email

Reports describe growing success of **voice (vishing) and SMS (smishing)** attacks, and IBM found vishing and smishing breaches carried the highest average cost in its sample ($5.29M). **Deepfake audio and video** now appear in executive-impersonation fraud.

### 5. AI Cuts Both Ways

| Offensive use | Defensive use |
|---------------|---------------|
| Fluent, personalized phishing at scale (ENISA cites reports that AI-supported phishing made up the large majority of observed social engineering activity by early 2025) | Alert triage and enrichment |
| Faster vulnerability discovery and exploit development | Anomaly and behavior detection |
| Deepfake voice and video for fraud | Automated containment (SOAR) |
| **Attacks on AI systems**: prompt injection, model inversion, data poisoning | Shortening breach lifecycle and cost |

**Shadow AI** (staff using unapproved AI tools with company data) is a new data-leakage risk that policy and DLP must address.

### 6. Supply Chain and Third-Party Risk

Compromising one supplier (a software vendor, managed service provider or open-source package) exposes all its customers. Your **attack surface includes your vendors**.

### 7. Cloud and Misconfiguration

Public storage buckets, over-permissioned identities, leaked access keys and exposed management consoles remain common causes of data exposure.

### 8. Hacktivism and State Activity Blend

Geopolitical events trigger waves of hacktivist DDoS and defacement, sometimes aligned with state interests, which blurs attribution.

## 1.4 Regional Context: East Africa

Students in Kenya and the wider region should also track:

- **Mobile money and SIM-swap fraud**, and fraud against agent networks.
- **Business Email Compromise** against SMEs and NGOs.
- **Ransomware and data theft** against government, health, education and finance.
- **Hacktivist DDoS** around political events.
- National guidance and incident coordination: Kenya's **National KE-CIRT/CC** publishes advisories and incident statistics, and the **Data Protection Act, 2019** and **Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act, 2018** set legal obligations. Check current advisories for local figures.

# PART 2: Types of Cyber Threats and Attack Vectors

You already know what a vector is. This section is the **catalogue**: what the main threat types are, how they typically arrive, what they damage, and what stops them.

## 2.1 Threat Taxonomy

| Category | Examples | Primary CIA impact |
|----------|----------|---------------------|
| **Malware** | Ransomware, wipers, info-stealers, remote access trojans (RATs), loaders, cryptominers, rootkits | C, I, A |
| **Social engineering** | Phishing, spear phishing, vishing, smishing, **BEC**, pretexting, baiting, deepfake fraud, MFA fatigue | C, I |
| **Credential attacks** | Password spraying, credential stuffing, brute force, Kerberoasting, pass-the-hash, session hijacking | C |
| **Web and application attacks** | SQL injection, XSS, broken access control, SSRF, insecure deserialization, API abuse | C, I |
| **Network attacks** | Man-in-the-middle, DNS spoofing/hijacking, rogue access points, port scanning, lateral movement | C, I |
| **Denial of service** | Volumetric, protocol and application-layer DDoS, resource exhaustion | A |
| **Supply chain** | Trojanized updates, poisoned packages, compromised MSPs and vendors | C, I, A |
| **Insider threats** | Malicious, negligent and compromised insiders | C, I, A |
| **Cloud threats** | Misconfiguration, leaked keys, over-privileged roles, tenant-level takeover | C, I, A |
| **Physical threats** | Theft, tailgating, malicious USB, rogue devices | C, I, A |
| **AI-related threats** | Prompt injection, model inversion, data poisoning, shadow AI leakage, deepfakes | C, I |
| **Mobile and telecom** | SIM swap, malicious apps, SMS interception, mobile money fraud | C, I |

> **Remember:** One incident usually chains several categories. A phishing email (social engineering) delivers an info-stealer (malware), whose stolen session token (credential attack) lets the attacker reach cloud storage (cloud threat) and exfiltrate data.

## 2.2 Deep Dives on Priority Threats

### Ransomware: Anatomy of a Modern Attack

| Stage | What happens | Defender opportunity |
|-------|--------------|----------------------|
| Initial access | Phished or stolen credentials, exploited edge device, or purchased access from a broker | Patch edge devices, phishing-resistant MFA, credential monitoring |
| Establish foothold | Remote access tool or beacon installed | EDR, application allow-listing |
| Privilege escalation and discovery | Credential dumping, Active Directory enumeration | Tiered admin, LSASS protection, honey accounts |
| Lateral movement | Remote services, valid accounts | Segmentation, east-west monitoring |
| Defense evasion | Disable security tools, clear logs | Tamper protection, off-host log forwarding |
| Exfiltration | Data staged and copied out | Egress filtering, DLP, DNS monitoring |
| Impact | Backups destroyed, encryption deployed, ransom note | **Immutable offline backups**, tested recovery |

**Key insight:** exfiltration and backup destruction happen **before** encryption. Detecting the quiet stages is far cheaper than recovering from the loud one.

### Phishing and Business Email Compromise

- **Phishing:** mass fraudulent messages. **Spear phishing:** targeted at a person using researched details.
- **BEC:** impersonating an executive or supplier to redirect payments. It often has **no malware at all**, so technical tools miss it. The effective control is a **procedure**: dual approval and call-back verification of payment changes.
- **Adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing** relays the login to the real site and steals the session cookie, defeating SMS and app-code MFA. **FIDO2/WebAuthn** resists it.

### Web Application Attacks

Map findings to the **OWASP Top 10** categories (broken access control, injection, cryptographic failures, security misconfiguration, vulnerable components and others). For a SOC, the practical signals are WAF alerts, error spikes, unusual query patterns and unexpected database writes.

### Insider Threats

| Type | Description | Typical indicators |
|------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Malicious** | Intentional theft, fraud or sabotage | Bulk downloads, access outside role, resignation followed by data movement |
| **Negligent** | Mistakes, policy violations, shadow IT | Misdirected email, data in personal cloud, unapproved tools |
| **Compromised** | Legitimate account used by an outsider | Impossible travel, new device, atypical hours |

Controls: least privilege, **UEBA**, DLP, strong offboarding, segregation of duties.

## 2.3 Attack Vectors: Mapping Threats to Entry Paths

| Vector | Typical threats delivered | Most effective controls |
|--------|---------------------------|-------------------------|
| **Exploited vulnerability** (internet-facing) | Ransomware, web shells, data theft | Asset inventory, patch SLAs (prioritize known-exploited flaws), virtual patching, segmentation |
| **Phishing / smishing / vishing** | Credential theft, malware, BEC | Awareness with reporting culture, email filtering, MFA, call-back procedures |
| **Stolen or reused credentials** | Account takeover, data theft | Phishing-resistant MFA, breach-credential monitoring, conditional access |
| **Third party / supply chain** | Trojanized updates, vendor-borne intrusion | Vendor due diligence, contract security clauses, SBOMs, least-privilege vendor access |
| **Misconfiguration** (cloud, services) | Data exposure | Posture management, infrastructure-as-code review, change control |
| **Insider** | Theft, sabotage | Least privilege, monitoring, offboarding |
| **Removable media / physical** | Malware, theft | Device control, encryption, physical security |
| **Remote access (RDP, VPN, SSH)** | Brute force, ransomware | Remove public exposure, MFA, VPN hardening |
| **Drive-by / malvertising** | Info-stealers, loaders | Browser isolation, DNS filtering, EDR |

## 2.4 Using Frameworks to Organize Threats

Use **MITRE ATT&CK** (tactics and techniques) to describe what adversaries do, **OWASP** for application weaknesses, and **STRIDE** for design-time threat modeling. (You have met ATT&CK already; here the point is to **tag every detection and risk scenario with a technique** so coverage gaps become visible.)

# PART 3: Threat Actors (Hacktivists, Nation-States, Cybercriminals)

You already have the definitions and comparison table. This part goes deeper on the three groups you were asked about: **how each operates, what they want, and how that should change your defenses.**

## 3.1 A Useful Lens: Motive, Means, Opportunity

Analysts assess an actor by three questions:

| Question | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| **Motive** | Why would they attack us? (money, ideology, strategic advantage) |
| **Means** | Do they have the skills, tools and infrastructure? |
| **Opportunity** | Do we expose a weakness they can reach? |

Your defenses address **opportunity**; you rarely influence motive or means.

## 3.2 Hacktivists

**Core motive:** ideology, protest, publicity.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Typical targets** | Governments, public administration, media, companies seen as supporting opposed causes |
| **Common methods** | DDoS, website defacement, doxxing, leaking documents obtained through opportunistic intrusion |
| **Organization** | Loose collectives, social-media coordinated campaigns, "crowd-sourced" tooling; some groups are **state-aligned** or even state-run under an activist persona |
| **Timing** | Spikes around elections, conflicts, sanctions and controversial announcements |
| **Impact profile** | **High volume, usually low impact.** ENISA's 2025 data shows hacktivist DDoS dominating incident counts while few caused real disruption |

**Defensive implications:**

- Know your **exposure to news cycles** (a company announcing a controversial contract is a target).
- Plan **DDoS resilience**: CDN or scrubbing service, rate limiting, runbooks for communicating with the public.
- Treat leaks and defacement as **reputational crises** with a communications plan, not only technical incidents.
- Do not ignore the minority of campaigns that go beyond DDoS (data theft, attacks on operational technology).

## 3.3 Nation-State Actors

**Core motive:** strategic advantage (espionage, political influence, economic gain, military preparation, sabotage), occasionally revenue generation for the state.

| Dimension | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Typical targets** | Government and diplomatic bodies, defense, telecoms, energy and other critical infrastructure, technology and research firms, journalists and dissidents |
| **Missions** | **Espionage** (long-term quiet collection), **pre-positioning** (hidden access held for use in a future crisis), **disruption or destruction** (wipers, infrastructure attacks), **influence operations** (disinformation, FIMI), and **revenue theft** by some state-linked groups |
| **Capabilities** | Zero-days, supply chain compromise, custom implants, living-off-the-land techniques, careful operational security, large teams with defined roles |
| **Persistence** | Months to years; re-entry after eviction |
| **Contractors and proxies** | Many states use private contractors, criminal groups or "hacktivist" personas, which complicates **attribution** |
| **Naming** | Vendors and governments name groups differently (APT numbers, animal or weather themes). Use **MITRE ATT&CK Groups** to cross-reference |

**Important cautions for analysts:**

- **Attribution is hard, slow and often political.** Do not attribute in an incident report without strong, multi-source evidence. Describe behavior (TTPs) first.
- Most organizations are not direct state targets, but can be hit **indirectly** through a supplier, or by **spillover** from destructive tools aimed at a specific country.
- State-linked and criminal activity can overlap (shared tools, shared infrastructure).

**Defensive implications:**

- Assume **breach is possible** and invest in detection and hunting, not only prevention.
- Prioritize **identity security, network segmentation, logging retention** (long dwell times need long memory), and supplier risk management.
- For critical sectors, join **information-sharing communities** and follow national CERT guidance.

## 3.4 Cybercriminals

**Core motive:** money.

### The Criminal Ecosystem

Cybercrime is a **supply chain of specialists**, which is why a relatively unskilled actor can launch a damaging attack.

| Role | What they do |
|------|--------------|
| **Malware developers** | Build and rent loaders, info-stealers, ransomware |
| **Info-stealer operators** | Harvest credentials and session tokens at scale |
| **Initial access brokers (IABs)** | Sell footholds (VPN credentials, remote desktop access) |
| **Ransomware affiliates** | Break in and deploy ransomware under a RaaS program |
| **Negotiators and leak-site operators** | Run the extortion |
| **Bulletproof hosting and infrastructure providers** | Host attack infrastructure that ignores abuse reports |
| **Money launderers and cashout services** | Convert cryptocurrency and stolen funds into usable money |
| **Fraud specialists** | BEC, invoice fraud, mobile-money and card fraud |

### How Criminals Choose Targets

Criminals optimize **return on effort**. They favor targets with: exposed vulnerable services, weak authentication, valuable or time-sensitive data (hospitals, schools, logistics), and cyber insurance that makes payment likely. This is **opportunistic** at the scanning stage and **targeted** ("big game hunting") once access is found.

**Defensive implications:**

- Raising the cost of attack works: **MFA, patching edge devices, offline backups and egress filtering** remove the easy wins.
- Monitor for **your credentials appearing in stealer logs or broker markets**.
- Rehearse the ransomware decision: who authorizes isolation, who talks to regulators, who decides on payment (legal, sanctions and law enforcement considerations apply).

## 3.5 Side-by-Side Planning View

| | **Hacktivists** | **Nation-states** | **Cybercriminals** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Visibility, protest | Strategic advantage | Profit |
| **Typical intensity** | High volume, mostly low impact | Low volume, potentially severe, long-lived | High volume, high impact when ransomware or fraud succeeds |
| **Stealth** | Low (they want attention) | Very high | Moderate (stealthy until extortion) |
| **Planning horizon** | Event-driven | Years | Weeks to months per campaign |
| **Key defensive emphasis** | DDoS resilience, communications | Detection, hunting, logging retention, supply chain | Patching, MFA, backups, egress control |
| **Best intel to follow** | News and geopolitics | Government and sector CERT advisories, ATT&CK Groups | Ransomware trackers, stealer-log monitoring |

## 3.6 Threat Actor Profiling Exercise

For each organization below, rank the three actor types by likelihood and say why: (a) a county hospital, (b) a regional telecom operator, (c) a mid-sized online retailer, (d) a national electoral body.

*Suggested reasoning:* the hospital faces **criminals** first (valuable, time-critical data); the telecom faces **nation-states and criminals** (strategic data, SIM-swap enablement); the retailer faces **criminals** (payment data, fraud); the electoral body faces **hacktivists and nation-states** (political salience).

# PART 4: Risk Analysis Methodologies

## 4.1 The Generic Risk Management Process

All major methods share this skeleton (compare ISO 31000, ISO/IEC 27005 and NIST SP 800-39):

```
        ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  COMMUNICATE & CONSULT     (throughout)                │
        │                                                        │
        │  1. Establish context  (scope, criteria, appetite)     │
        │  2. RISK ASSESSMENT                                    │
        │       a. Identification (assets, threats, vulns)       │
        │       b. Analysis (likelihood x impact)                │
        │       c. Evaluation (compare with criteria, prioritize)│
        │  3. Risk treatment  (select options, plan)             │
        │  4. Accept residual risk (risk owner sign-off)         │
        │  5. MONITOR & REVIEW     (feeds back into step 1)      │
        └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 4.2 Two Ways to Identify Risk

| Approach | Starting point | Strength | Weakness |
|----------|----------------|----------|----------|
| **Asset-based** | List assets, then find threats and vulnerabilities for each | Systematic, aligns with asset inventory | Can produce huge, generic lists; may miss scenarios that span assets |
| **Event (scenario)-based** | Start from realistic **events** ("ransomware encrypts file servers via compromised VPN") and work backward | Business-relevant, links to threat intelligence, easier to explain to executives | May miss unusual assets; depends on analyst imagination |

ISO/IEC 27005:2022 explicitly recognizes both approaches and allows combining them. A practical blend: **use scenarios for the top risks and an asset-based review for completeness.**

## 4.3 Overview of Well-Known Methodologies

| Methodology | Origin | Character | Best for |
|-------------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **ISO/IEC 27005:2022** | ISO/IEC | Process guidance supporting an ISMS (ISO 27001); method-agnostic | Organizations pursuing or maintaining ISO 27001 |
| **NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1** | US NIST | Detailed assessment method (threat sources, events, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact); qualitative or semi-quantitative scales | Government and regulated sectors; strong worked guidance |
| **NIST RMF (SP 800-37 Rev. 2)** | US NIST | Seven-step lifecycle for authorizing and managing systems | Federal systems and contractors; useful template elsewhere |
| **FAIR (Factor Analysis of Information Risk)** | Open Group standard | **Quantitative** model of loss event frequency and loss magnitude, in money | Executive decisions, cyber insurance, cost-benefit of controls |
| **OCTAVE / OCTAVE Allegro** | Carnegie Mellon SEI | Asset and operations-focused, workshop-based self-assessment | Organizations wanting business-owner involvement |
| **EBIOS Risk Manager** | French national agency (ANSSI) | Scenario-based, strategic and operational scenarios | European public and critical sectors |
| **CRAMM** | UK origin | Asset-valuation-driven, structured | Legacy and government use |
| **Threat modeling** (STRIDE, PASTA, attack trees) | Microsoft, others | Design-time analysis of a specific system | Application and architecture review |
| **FMEA / bow-tie** | Engineering and safety | Failure-mode and cause-consequence diagrams | Safety-critical and OT environments |

> **Choosing a method:** Match to **purpose, maturity and data**. A small organization with little loss data may do well with a **qualitative 5x5** method tied to ISO 27005 or NIST 800-30. A mature organization facing a board question ("how much should we spend?") benefits from **FAIR-style quantification** on the top risks.

## 4.4 Building a Risk Register

The **risk register** is the living record of risk decisions.

| Field | Purpose |
|-------|---------|
| **Risk ID and title** | Unique reference |
| **Scenario description** | Threat + vulnerability + asset + consequence, written as a sentence |
| **Risk owner** | The **accountable** person with authority over the asset or process (not necessarily the security team) |
| **Existing controls** | What already reduces the risk |
| **Inherent / current / target rating** | Before controls, now, and goal |
| **Treatment decision and plan** | Option chosen, actions, owner, deadline, cost |
| **Residual rating and acceptance** | Remaining risk and who accepted it |
| **KRIs** | Key Risk Indicators that signal change (for example, patch backlog on internet-facing systems) |
| **Review date** | When it will be revisited |

### Worked Register Example (5x5 Qualitative)

Scale: Likelihood 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain); Impact 1 (negligible) to 5 (severe). Score = L x I. Bands: **1-4 Low, 5-9 Medium, 10-16 High, 17-25 Critical.**

| ID | Risk scenario | L | I | Score | Rating | Treatment idea |
|----|---------------|---|---|-------|--------|----------------|
| R1 | Ransomware encrypts file servers after attacker exploits an unpatched VPN appliance | 4 | 5 | 20 | **Critical** | Patch and harden VPN, MFA, immutable backups, segmentation |
| R2 | BEC fraud redirects a supplier payment | 4 | 3 | 12 | **High** | Dual approval, call-back verification, training |
| R3 | Departing sales employee copies customer list to personal cloud | 3 | 3 | 9 | **Medium** | DLP, offboarding checklist, access review |
| R4 | DDoS makes the public website unavailable for hours | 3 | 2 | 6 | **Medium** | CDN/scrubbing, runbook |
| R5 | Unencrypted laptop with client data is lost | 3 | 3 | 9 | **Medium** | Full-disk encryption (after treatment: L3 x I1 = 3, **Low**) |

### Risk Appetite, Tolerance and Capacity

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Risk appetite** | The *amount and type* of risk the organization is willing to pursue for its objectives | "We accept low risk on customer data and moderate risk on new product launches" |
| **Risk tolerance** | The acceptable *variation* around objectives; usually expressed as thresholds | "No single event above $500,000 loss; recovery within 8 hours for core systems" |
| **Risk capacity** | The maximum risk the organization can bear before survival is threatened | Determined by capital, insurance, regulatory standing |

Risk evaluation compares each scored risk with these criteria to decide whether treatment is needed.

# PART 5: Qualitative vs Quantitative Risk Analysis

## 5.1 The Spectrum

```
QUALITATIVE ───────────── SEMI-QUANTITATIVE ───────────── QUANTITATIVE
(High / Medium / Low)      (Scores 1-5, bands, ranges)     (Money and probabilities)
```

## 5.2 Comparison

| Aspect | **Qualitative** | **Semi-quantitative** | **Quantitative** |
|--------|-----------------|-----------------------|------------------|
| **Output** | Ratings (Low to Critical), heat map | Numeric scores mapped to bands | Expected and range-of-loss in currency, probabilities |
| **Inputs** | Expert judgment, workshops | Scales with defined meanings (for example impact ranges in money) | Loss data, frequency estimates, calibrated expert ranges |
| **Effort and skill** | Low to moderate | Moderate | High |
| **Speed** | Fast | Moderate | Slower |
| **Precision** | Low; ratings are ordinal | Medium | High **if** inputs are good; otherwise false precision |
| **Communication** | Easy to grasp | Easy | Powerful for executives, but needs explanation of uncertainty |
| **Subjectivity** | High | Medium | Lower in method, but inputs still rely on estimates |
| **Supports cost-benefit?** | Weakly | Somewhat | **Strongly** |
| **Typical tools** | Risk matrix, workshops, NIST 800-30 qualitative scales | NIST 800-30 semi-quantitative scale, scored registers | FAIR, SLE/ALE, Monte Carlo simulation |
| **Best used for** | Broad screening of many risks; small or early-stage programs | Prioritizing a large register consistently | Top risks, major investments, insurance, board reporting |

## 5.3 Pitfalls of Qualitative and Scored Methods

- **Range compression:** very different risks land in the same band.
- **Ordinal math is misleading:** a "4 x 3 = 12" is not twice as bad as "3 x 2 = 6". The scores are labels, not measurements.
- **Ambiguous words:** "likely" means 20% to one person and 80% to another. **Define each level** (for example Likely = more than once per 3 years; Impact 4 = loss of $250k-$1M).
- **Optimism and anchoring biases** in workshops.
- **Same score, different decisions:** a high-likelihood, low-impact risk and a low-likelihood, catastrophic one can both score 10, yet need different treatment.

## 5.4 Quantitative Analysis Beyond SLE/ALE

You have already used **SLE x ARO = ALE**. It is a useful classroom model with **limits**:

- It reduces everything to **single-point estimates**, hiding uncertainty.
- It assumes a **fixed loss per event**, though real losses vary enormously (a few small incidents and rare catastrophes).
- It gives an **average**, but executives often care about the **bad year**.

### FAIR in Brief

FAIR breaks risk into measurable parts:

```
RISK  =  LOSS EVENT FREQUENCY  x  LOSS MAGNITUDE

LEF = Threat Event Frequency x Vulnerability (probability a threat event becomes a loss)
Loss Magnitude = Primary losses (response, replacement, productivity)
               + Secondary losses (fines, reputation, litigation)
```

Inputs are **calibrated ranges** (minimum, most likely, maximum) rather than single numbers, and the result is a **distribution**.

### Worked Example: Monte Carlo Simulation

**Scenario:** Ransomware on the file-server estate (risk R1 above).

**Estimates (from threat intelligence, past incidents and expert ranges):**

- **Frequency:** an average of **0.30** damaging ransomware events per year (roughly one every three to four years). Modeled as a Poisson process.
- **Severity per event:** typical (median) loss **$250,000**, with a **90th percentile of $1.5 million** (a bad event is much worse than a typical one). Modeled as a lognormal distribution fitted to those two points.

**Method:** simulate 100,000 independent years. In each year, draw the number of events, then draw a loss for each event and sum.

**Results of the simulation (100,000 simulated years):**

| Measure | Result |
|---------|--------|
| Probability of **no loss** in a year | about **74%** |
| **Mean (expected) annual loss** | about **$202,000** |
| **90th percentile** annual loss | about **$460,000** |
| **95th percentile** annual loss | about **$1.02 million** |
| **99th percentile** annual loss | about **$3.4 million** |
| Probability that annual loss exceeds **$250,000** | about **14%** |
| Probability that annual loss exceeds **$1 million** | about **5%** |
| Probability that annual loss exceeds **$3 million** | about **1%** |

> The simulation uses illustrative inputs for teaching. The point is the **shape** of the answer, not the numbers.

**How to read it:**

- In **three years out of four** nothing happens, which is why risk is easy to ignore.
- The **average ($202k)** is a poor guide to the **bad year**: in 1 year in 20 the loss exceeds about $1 million, and in 1 year in 100 it exceeds roughly $3.4 million.
- A single "ALE" would hide this **long tail**, which is exactly what insurance, reserves and resilience planning must address.

**Evaluating a control (analytical check):**

For a lognormal severity, the mean per event is approximately `median x e^(sigma squared / 2)`, which for these inputs is about **$664,000**. Expected annual loss is therefore roughly `frequency x mean severity`, about 0.30 x 664,000 = **$199,000** (matching the simulation).

Suppose a $50,000 per year control (immutable backups, patching program, segmentation) cuts event frequency from 0.30 to about 0.10:

- New expected loss ≈ 0.10 x 664,000 ≈ **$66,000**.
- Reduction ≈ **$133,000** per year.
- **Return on Security Investment (ROSI)** = (Risk reduction − Cost) / Cost = (133,000 − 50,000) / 50,000 ≈ **1.66 (166%)**.

A control that **also** reduces severity (for example, immutable backups shorten recovery) would show an even better result when modeled.

### Honest Limits of Quantification

- Garbage in, garbage out. Cyber loss data is sparse; ranges come from **calibrated expert estimates** and benchmarks.
- Models can create **false confidence**. Always show ranges, state assumptions and test sensitivity (what happens if frequency is double?).
- A pragmatic approach: **qualitative to screen the whole register, quantitative on the top five to ten risks.**

## 5.5 Choosing Between Them: Decision Guide

| Situation | Recommended approach |
|-----------|----------------------|
| First risk assessment, many assets, limited data | Qualitative or semi-quantitative with **defined scales** |
| Large register needing consistent prioritization | Semi-quantitative scoring |
| Board asks "how much should we spend and what do we get?" | Quantitative (FAIR-style) on top risks |
| Insurance limit setting | Quantitative, focusing on tail percentiles |
| Design review of a new system | Threat modeling plus qualitative scoring |

# PART 6: Risk Assessment Frameworks (NIST and ISO 27005)

## 6.1 The NIST Family

NIST publishes several connected documents. Students often confuse them, so here is the map.

| Document | What it is | Role |
|----------|------------|------|
| **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0** (2024) | Outcome-based framework with six Functions: **Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover** | Organizes and communicates a cybersecurity program; **not** a control checklist or an assessment method |
| **NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1** (2012) | *Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments* | The **assessment method** (how to identify and rate risk) |
| **NIST SP 800-39** | *Managing Information Security Risk* | The **enterprise risk management** view across three tiers |
| **NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2** | *Risk Management Framework (RMF)* | A **lifecycle** for categorizing, selecting controls, authorizing and monitoring systems |
| **NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5** (with FIPS 199 and 200) | Security and privacy **control catalogue** and categorization standards | The **controls** to select |
| **NIST IR 8286 series** (revised December 2025) | Connects cybersecurity risk to **Enterprise Risk Management** (risk registers, aggregation, reporting) | Bridges security and corporate risk |

> **How they work together:** CSF tells you *what outcomes* to aim for, SP 800-30 tells you *how to assess risk*, RMF tells you *how to manage a system through its life*, and SP 800-53 gives the *controls*. IR 8286 helps you report it all to enterprise risk leaders.

### Three-Tier Risk Management (SP 800-39)

| Tier | Level | Concerns |
|------|-------|----------|
| **Tier 1** | Organization | Governance, risk appetite, strategy, policies |
| **Tier 2** | Mission / business process | Enterprise architecture, shared services, process-level risk |
| **Tier 3** | Information system | System-level controls and assessment |

Risk decisions and information flow **up and down** these tiers.

### SP 800-39 Risk Management Cycle

**Frame** (set context, assumptions, appetite) → **Assess** → **Respond** → **Monitor**.

### SP 800-30 Risk Assessment Process

SP 800-30 describes four steps:

| Step | Activities |
|------|------------|
| **1. Prepare** | Define purpose, scope, assumptions, constraints, information sources and the analytic approach |
| **2. Conduct** | Identify **threat sources and events**; identify **vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions**; determine **likelihood**; determine **impact**; determine **risk** |
| **3. Communicate** | Share results and supporting evidence with decision-makers |
| **4. Maintain** | Keep the assessment current through ongoing monitoring |

**Key features:**

- Distinguishes **threat source** (the actor or cause) from **threat event** (what they do).
- Supports **threat-oriented**, **asset/impact-oriented** and **vulnerability-oriented** analysis.
- Provides **qualitative** (Very Low to Very High) and **semi-quantitative** scales (for example, a 0-100 scale mapped to the same bands) with example tables you can adapt.
- Treats likelihood as a combination of the likelihood an event **is initiated** and the likelihood it **succeeds** in causing harm.

### Risk Management Framework (SP 800-37 Rev. 2): Seven Steps

| Step | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Prepare** | Establish roles, risk strategy and organization-wide readiness |
| **Categorize** | Classify the system and its information by impact (low, moderate, high) |
| **Select** | Choose and tailor controls from SP 800-53 |
| **Implement** | Deploy the controls and document how |
| **Assess** | Test whether controls work as intended |
| **Authorize** | A senior official formally accepts the residual risk (**authorization to operate**) |
| **Monitor** | Continuously monitor controls and changes |

### CSF 2.0 in Practice

- **Govern** is new in 2.0 and covers context, strategy, roles, policy and supply chain risk oversight.
- **Profiles** describe a **Current** and **Target** state; the **gap** becomes your improvement plan.
- **Tiers** (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) describe the rigor of risk management practices.
- The **Risk Assessment (ID.RA)** category in Identify is where methods such as SP 800-30 plug in.

## 6.2 ISO/IEC 27005:2022

### Position in the ISO Family

| Standard | Role |
|----------|------|
| **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** | **Requirements** for an Information Security Management System (ISMS); Clause 6.1 requires risk assessment and treatment; certifiable |
| **ISO/IEC 27002:2022** | **Control guidance** (the detail behind Annex A) |
| **ISO/IEC 27005:2022** | **Guidance on managing information security risks**; supports 27001 Clause 6.1; **not certifiable by itself** |
| **ISO 31000:2018** | Generic enterprise **risk management principles and process**, which 27005 applies to information security |
| **ISO/IEC 27003, 27004** | ISMS implementation guidance and measurement |

ISO/IEC 27005:2022 is the **fourth edition** (published October 2022). It replaced the 2018 edition and was re-aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 terminology and structure.

### What Changed in the 2022 Edition (Key Points)

- Title and scope updated to **information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection**.
- Two explicit **risk identification approaches**: **event-based** (scenario) and **asset-based**.
- A clause on **acceptance of the residual information security risk**, with emphasis on the **risk owner** approving treatment plans.
- Closer alignment between **treatment decisions, ISO 27001 Annex A controls and the Statement of Applicability (SoA)**.
- Clearer separation of the **risk management process** from the **risk management cycles** (operational and strategic, repeated over time).

### The ISO/IEC 27005 Process

| Activity | What it involves |
|----------|------------------|
| **Context establishment** | Scope and boundaries, risk criteria (impact, likelihood, **risk acceptance criteria**), organizational roles |
| **Information security risk assessment** | Identification (assets, threats, vulnerabilities, existing controls, consequences), **analysis** (qualitative or quantitative), **evaluation** against criteria |
| **Information security risk treatment** | Select options, determine **all controls necessary**, compare with ISO 27001 Annex A to confirm nothing is omitted, produce the **SoA** and a **risk treatment plan** |
| **Acceptance of residual risk** | Risk owners approve the plan and **formally accept residual risk** |
| **Communication and consultation** | Continuous engagement with stakeholders |
| **Monitoring and review** | Track risks, controls and the environment; update assessments |

### Linking Risk to the ISMS Documents

```
RISK ASSESSMENT  ──►  RISK TREATMENT PLAN  ──►  STATEMENT OF APPLICABILITY (SoA)
(what could go wrong)  (what we will do)        (which Annex A controls apply, and why;
                                                 which are excluded and why)
          ▲                                                      │
          └──────────────── monitoring, audit, review ◄──────────┘
```

**ISO 27001:2022 Annex A** contains **93 controls in four themes**: Organizational (37), People (8), Physical (14) and Technological (34).

## 6.3 NIST vs ISO 27005: Comparison

| Aspect | **NIST (SP 800-30 / 800-37 / CSF 2.0)** | **ISO/IEC 27005:2022** |
|--------|----------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Nature** | Detailed, prescriptive method plus control catalogue; freely available | Process guidance; purchased standard |
| **Certification** | No certification of the framework itself (CSF); RMF authorization for federal systems | 27005 not certifiable; **ISO 27001 certification** is the audit target |
| **Control set** | SP 800-53 (hundreds of controls, baselines by impact level) | ISO 27002 / Annex A (93 controls) |
| **Risk method detail** | Very detailed scales, tables and examples | Flexible, method-agnostic; you choose techniques |
| **Typical user** | US federal bodies, contractors, regulated industries, and many others voluntarily | Organizations seeking international certification and customer assurance |
| **Governance emphasis** | Three-tier model, authorizing official | Risk owners, management commitment, SoA |
| **Interoperability** | CSF maps to ISO 27001 and many other references | ISO controls map to NIST and others |

> **Practical advice:** They are **complementary**. Many organizations use **ISO 27001/27005** for the management system and certification, **NIST SP 800-30 scales** for detailed analysis, and **CSF 2.0** to communicate maturity to executives.

## 6.4 Worked Mini-Case: Applying Both

**Case:** A regional microfinance company stores borrower data and runs a mobile loan app.

1. **Context (both):** scope = loan platform, support staff, two cloud environments; acceptance criteria = no residual risk above "Medium" without board approval.
2. **Identify (event-based):** "Attacker uses phished staff credentials to access the loan database and sell borrower data."
3. **Analyze (SP 800-30 style):** threat source = cybercriminal (capability: moderate, intent: financial); vulnerability = SMS-based MFA and no data-access monitoring; likelihood **High**; impact **High** (regulatory exposure under data protection law, reputational harm) → risk **High**.
4. **Evaluate (ISO 27005):** exceeds acceptance criteria, so treatment is required.
5. **Treat:** adopt phishing-resistant MFA, least-privilege access to the database, DLP and anomaly alerts, and cyber insurance for residual financial exposure.
6. **Map controls:** record in the **SoA** which ISO 27001 Annex A controls apply; map to **CSF** outcomes for the board scorecard.
7. **Accept residual risk:** the risk owner (head of lending) signs the acceptance of the new **Medium** residual rating.
8. **Monitor:** KRIs (phishing report rate, privileged access exceptions); review quarterly.

# PART 7: Risk Treatment Strategies

You met the four options earlier. Here is how treatment is **decided, documented, funded and accepted** in practice.

## 7.1 The Options, With Decision Criteria

| Option | Use when | Typical cyber actions | Watch out for |
|--------|----------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Mitigate (modify)** | Risk exceeds appetite and a cost-effective control exists | Patching, MFA, segmentation, backups, monitoring, training, policy | Controls fail; reassess residual risk |
| **Transfer / share** | Financial impact is large and can be insured or contracted out | Cyber insurance, outsourcing, contractual indemnities | **Accountability for data and reputation stays with you**; policies have exclusions, conditions (for example required MFA) and limits |
| **Avoid** | The activity's risk outweighs its value | Retire a legacy system, do not collect unneeded data, exit a risky market | Business opportunity cost |
| **Accept (retain)** | Risk is within appetite, or treatment costs more than the exposure | Documented acceptance with an owner and review date | Acceptance must be **explicit, approved by the right authority and time-limited**, never a default by neglect |

NIST uses **accept, avoid, mitigate, share or transfer**; ISO/IEC 27005 uses **modify, retain, avoid, share**. They mean the same things.

## 7.2 Treatment Decision Logic

```
Is the risk above acceptance criteria?
 ├─ No ─► Accept (document, monitor, set review date)
 └─ Yes ─► Can we stop the activity cheaply?  ── Yes ─► AVOID
            │ No
            ▼
          Is there a cost-effective control?   ── Yes ─► MITIGATE
            │ No / partial                              (then re-rate residual risk)
            ▼
          Is there large, insurable financial exposure left? ─ Yes ─► TRANSFER / SHARE the remainder
            │
            ▼
          Is the remaining residual risk within appetite?
             ├─ Yes ─► ACCEPT (formal sign-off by risk owner)
             └─ No ─► Escalate to senior management / board, or reconsider AVOID
```

Real treatments usually **combine options** (mitigate most of it, insure the tail, accept the rest).

## 7.3 Choosing Controls: Cost-Effectiveness

1. List candidate controls for the risk.
2. Estimate **risk reduction** (change in likelihood, impact or both).
3. Estimate **total cost**: purchase, implementation, running cost, staff time and user friction.
4. Compare using **ROSI** or a simple risk-reduction-per-dollar ranking.
5. Consider **dependencies and side effects** (a control may create new risks, as with centralizing credentials).
6. Prefer **layered** controls (preventive + detective + corrective), as in defense in depth.

**Control categories to balance:** preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating; and by nature: administrative (policy), technical, physical.

## 7.4 The Risk Treatment Plan

A good plan states, per risk:

| Element | Example |
|---------|---------|
| **Chosen option(s)** | Mitigate plus transfer |
| **Controls and actions** | Deploy FIDO2 keys for all staff with remote access; immutable backups; segment file servers |
| **Owner and resources** | Head of IT; budget approved |
| **Timeline and milestones** | Phase 1 in 60 days; complete in 6 months |
| **Expected residual risk** | From Critical (20) to Medium (8) |
| **Success measures / KRIs** | % of staff with security keys; backup restore test pass rate |
| **Approval** | Risk owner signature |

## 7.5 Residual Risk Acceptance

**Residual risk** is what remains after treatment. Mature organizations govern acceptance carefully:

- **Who can accept what:** a tiered authority matrix, for example Low risks by the system owner, Medium by a department head, High by an executive, Critical by the board or CEO.
- **Risk owner is accountable;** the security team **advises** and does not own business risk.
- **Time-limit acceptances** and review them. Unreviewed acceptances become hidden liabilities.
- **Risk exceptions** (an asset that cannot meet policy) need a documented justification, **compensating controls** and an expiry date.
- **Never accept silently.** "We did nothing" is not acceptance; it is unmanaged risk.

## 7.6 Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) and Ongoing Monitoring

| KRI | What it signals |
|-----|-----------------|
| Days to patch internet-facing critical vulnerabilities | Exposure to the top initial access vector |
| % of accounts with phishing-resistant MFA | Credential-attack resilience |
| Backup restore test success rate | Recovery capability against ransomware |
| Phishing simulation report rate (not just click rate) | Security culture health |
| Number of open risk exceptions past expiry | Governance discipline |
| Mean time to detect / respond | SOC effectiveness |

## 7.7 Cyber Insurance: What Students Should Know

- It **transfers financial loss**, not responsibility, and does not repair reputation.
- Insurers increasingly **require baseline controls** (MFA, backups, EDR) and may reduce or deny cover if representations are inaccurate.
- Policies have **limits, retentions (deductibles) and exclusions**, including for some state-linked or war-related events. **Read the wording.**
- Insurance pairs well with the **tail-risk** view from quantitative analysis: use simulation percentiles to help set limits.

# PART 8: Information Security Policies and Standards

## 8.1 Why Policies Matter

Policies convert **management intent** into **enforceable rules**. They are the foundation of the **Process** pillar and the evidence auditors and regulators ask for first. Under ISO 27001, an information security policy approved by management is a **mandatory** document.

> **Analogy:** The law says "drive safely" (policy); the highway code sets speed limits and signals (standards); a driving instructor explains how to parallel park (procedure); a friend's advice on the best route is a guideline.

## 8.2 The Policy Document Hierarchy

| Document | Nature | Mandatory? | Answers | Example |
|----------|--------|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Policy** | High-level statement of intent and rules, approved by senior management | **Yes** | **Why** and **what** | "All confidential data must be encrypted in storage and transit" |
| **Standard** | Specific, measurable **requirements** supporting a policy | **Yes** | **What exactly** | "Use AES-256 for data at rest and TLS 1.2 or higher in transit" |
| **Baseline** | Minimum secure configuration for a platform | **Yes** | **What settings** | "CIS-aligned hardened Windows Server build" |
| **Procedure** | Step-by-step instructions to perform a task | **Yes** (for the task) | **How** | "Steps to onboard a new user and assign roles" |
| **Guideline** | Recommended good practice | **No** | **How best** | "Tips for choosing a strong passphrase" |

```
          POLICY        (rare changes, board / executive approval)
             │
          STANDARDS     (change when technology or risk changes)
             │
   BASELINES  &  PROCEDURES   (detailed, change often)
             │
          GUIDELINES    (advice)
```

## 8.3 Common Information Security Policies

| Policy | Purpose |
|--------|---------|
| **Information Security Policy (master)** | Management commitment, scope, objectives, roles; the umbrella for the rest |
| **Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)** | What staff may and may not do with company systems, email, internet and AI tools |
| **Access Control Policy** | Least privilege, authentication, joiner-mover-leaver, privileged access |
| **Password / Authentication Standard** | Length, MFA requirements, lockout, storage |
| **Data Classification and Handling Policy** | Labels and handling rules (builds on the four-tier model) |
| **Data Protection and Privacy Policy** | Lawful processing, data subject rights, breach notification |
| **Cryptography Policy** | Approved algorithms, key management |
| **Incident Response Policy and Plan** | Roles, severity levels, reporting and escalation |
| **Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Policy** | RTO, RPO, backups, testing |
| **Backup Policy** | What, how often, where, retention, restore testing |
| **Remote Work / BYOD / Mobile Device Policy** | Device security and personal device use |
| **Network Security Policy** | Segmentation, firewall management, remote access |
| **Vulnerability and Patch Management Standard** | Scan frequency, patch SLAs by severity |
| **Secure Development Policy** | Coding standards, testing, change control |
| **Third-Party / Supplier Security Policy** | Due diligence, contract clauses, monitoring |
| **Logging and Monitoring Standard** | What to log, retention, review |
| **Physical Security Policy** | Facilities, visitors, equipment, clear desk |
| **Generative AI Use Policy** | Approved tools, data that must never be entered, review of outputs (responds directly to shadow AI risk) |

## 8.4 Anatomy of a Good Policy

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Title, version, owner, approval date, review date** | Governance metadata |
| **Purpose** | Why the policy exists |
| **Scope** | Who and what it applies to (employees, contractors, systems) |
| **Policy statements** | Clear, unambiguous "must" and "must not" rules |
| **Roles and responsibilities** | Who does what |
| **Compliance and enforcement** | Monitoring and consequences of violation |
| **Exceptions process** | How to request a time-limited exception |
| **Related documents and references** | Linked standards, procedures, laws |
| **Definitions** | Key terms |
| **Revision history** | Change tracking |

### Writing Tips

- Use **plain language** and short sentences; aim for rules people can follow under pressure.
- Use **"must"** for mandatory, **"should"** for recommended; avoid vague words like "appropriate" without a standard behind them.
- Write **policy** at a level that rarely changes; put **technology details** in standards.
- Make each rule **auditable**: someone should be able to show evidence of compliance.

### Short Sample Policy Extract

> **Remote Access Policy (extract)**
> **Purpose:** To protect company systems and data accessed from outside the office network.
> **Scope:** All employees, contractors and third parties using remote access.
> **Statements:**
> 1. Remote access **must** use the approved VPN or zero-trust access service.
> 2. All remote access **must** use multi-factor authentication.
> 3. Devices used for remote access **must** meet the Endpoint Security Standard.
> 4. Confidential data **must not** be saved to unmanaged personal devices.
> 5. Suspected compromise **must** be reported to the Security Operations Center within one hour.
> **Exceptions:** Approved in writing by the Head of IT Security, valid for no more than 90 days.
> **Enforcement:** Violations may lead to access suspension and disciplinary action.

## 8.5 The Policy Lifecycle

```
DEVELOP ► APPROVE ► PUBLISH & TRAIN ► IMPLEMENT ► MONITOR & ENFORCE ► REVIEW & UPDATE
   ▲                                                                          │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Phase | Key practices |
|-------|---------------|
| **Develop** | Base on risk assessment and legal requirements; involve stakeholders (legal, HR, IT, business owners) |
| **Approve** | Senior management sign-off signals commitment |
| **Publish and train** | Make policies easy to find; train on the ones that affect each role; obtain acknowledgment |
| **Implement** | Support with technical controls so compliance is the easy path |
| **Monitor and enforce** | Audits, automated compliance checks, consistent consequences |
| **Review and update** | At least annually and after major incidents, technology changes or new laws |

**Why policies fail:** written once and never reviewed; too long to read; no enforcement; contradict how work is actually done (driving staff to workarounds); no executive backing.

## 8.6 Standards, Frameworks and Regulations

**Policies are shaped by external requirements.** Know the difference:

| Type | Examples | Nature |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Management system standards** | ISO/IEC 27001:2022 | Certifiable requirements for running an ISMS |
| **Control guidance** | ISO/IEC 27002:2022, NIST SP 800-53, **CIS Critical Security Controls** | Catalogues of controls to adopt |
| **Frameworks** | NIST CSF 2.0 | Outcome-based structure for programs |
| **Industry standards** | **PCI DSS** (payment cards) | Mandated by contract for card handlers |
| **Laws and regulations** | GDPR, HIPAA, Kenya Data Protection Act 2019, Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act 2018, sector rules (central bank, communications regulator) | **Legally binding** |

> **Hierarchy of authority:** **Law** overrides contract (**PCI DSS**), which overrides **voluntary standards**, which inform **your internal policy**. Your policies should **map to** each applicable external requirement.

### ISO/IEC 27002:2022 Control Themes (Useful Policy Skeleton)

| Theme | Number of controls | Policy topics it suggests |
|-------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| **Organizational** | 37 | Policies, roles, asset management, access, supplier relationships, incident management, continuity, legal compliance |
| **People** | 8 | Screening, terms of employment, awareness, disciplinary process, remote working |
| **Physical** | 14 | Secure areas, clear desk, equipment security, secure disposal |
| **Technological** | 34 | Endpoint security, privileged access, encryption, logging, vulnerability management, secure development, network security |

## 8.7 Policy and Risk Together

Risk management and policy are two halves of one system:

- **Risk assessment decides what rules are needed** (risk-based policy).
- **Policy sets the baseline** that reduces common risks consistently.
- **Exceptions** are **risk acceptances** and belong in the risk register.
- **Audit findings and incidents** feed back into both.

## 9.1 Integrated Case Study: Lakeview County Hospital

**Background:** A 300-bed county hospital runs an electronic patient records system, a patient portal, networked medical devices and a small IT team with no formal risk process. Staff use personal phones for messaging patient information. The VPN appliance is two years behind on patches.

**Tasks:**

1. **Threat landscape (Part 1):** Which two trends from the 2025-2026 reports apply most directly? Explain.
2. **Threat types and vectors (Part 2):** Identify three threat types and the vector for each.
3. **Actors (Part 3):** Rank hacktivists, nation-states and cybercriminals for this hospital and justify the order.
4. **Risk identification (Part 4):** Write three event-based risk scenarios and enter them in a 5x5 risk register with an owner for each.
5. **Analysis (Part 5):** For the top risk, state what data you would need to run a quantitative analysis and what you would estimate as frequency and severity ranges.
6. **Framework (Part 6):** Show how you would use ISO/IEC 27005 and NIST SP 800-30 together, and where CSF 2.0 Profiles help.
7. **Treatment (Part 7):** For each risk choose treatment options, state expected residual risk, and say who must accept it.
8. **Policy (Part 8):** List five policies the hospital needs first and draft the statements for one (for example the Mobile Device and Messaging Policy).

**Indicative answer points:**

- Trends: **exploitation of vulnerabilities** (unpatched VPN), **ransomware as an industry** targeting health; also **shadow IT/AI** (personal phone messaging).
- Top risk: ransomware via VPN exploitation; healthcare is a high-cost sector in IBM's data, and time-critical operations raise the chance of payment.
- Treatment: patch and harden VPN, MFA, segmentation of medical devices, immutable backups and tested recovery, approved secure messaging; **residual risk** of a clinical-system outage accepted only by executive leadership, with downtime procedures (paper-based care plans) as compensating controls.
- Policies: Information Security (master), Access Control, Mobile Device and Messaging, Incident Response, Backup and Recovery, Third-Party, Data Protection.

## Key Takeaways

- Threat reports are **snapshots with different methods**; use them for direction, and read definitions before comparing numbers.
- **Vulnerability exploitation, stolen identities, ransomware-as-an-industry, social engineering beyond email, AI, and supply chain risk** define the current landscape.
- Different actors need different emphasis: **hacktivists** (resilience and communications), **nation-states** (detection, hunting, supply chain), **cybercriminals** (patching, MFA, backups, egress control).
- Risk analysis follows a common process: **context, identify, analyze, evaluate, treat, accept, monitor.** Choose the method to fit your purpose, maturity and data.
- **Qualitative** methods screen broadly; **quantitative** methods (FAIR, Monte Carlo) support spending decisions and reveal the **tail risk** that averages hide.
- **NIST** (CSF 2.0, SP 800-30, RMF, SP 800-53) and **ISO/IEC 27005:2022** are **complementary**; ISO 27001 certification and the Statement of Applicability connect risk to controls.
- Treatment decisions combine **mitigate, transfer, avoid and accept**; residual risk acceptance must be **explicit, owned, authorized and time-limited**.
- **Policies** express management intent; **standards, baselines and procedures** make it specific and actionable; **enforcement and review** make it real.
